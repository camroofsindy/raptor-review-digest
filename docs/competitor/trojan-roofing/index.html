1. Who They Are

Trojan Roofing has been in operation since 2019

, making them a roughly six-year-old company.

The business is incorporated and owned by Tyler Morgan, operating out of 333 Alabama St Ste#350, Indianapolis, IN 46204.

Their scope extends beyond roofing to include siding and gutters, and they position themselves as authorized installers of IKO Dynasty and Owens Corning systems.

Their public-facing brand leans heavily on storm-damage insurance replacement work and high-volume door-to-door canvassing — a volume play, not a premium one.

2. Estimated Scale

Indeed estimates salary data drawn from approximately 69 employees

, though that figure reflects a mix of current and past job advertisement data and should be treated as an upper bound on active headcount. Confidence: low. No verified LinkedIn headcount or ZoomInfo revenue figure was findable.

Their BuildZoom score of 98 ranks them in the top 16% of 22,324 Indiana licensed contractors

— a signal of permit activity but not a revenue proxy. Given their storm-chasing model, door-to-door sales infrastructure, and 638 reviews accumulated since 2019, a rough estimate of $3M–$6M annual revenue is plausible. Revenue confidence: low.

3. Recent Hiring Signals

Active and ongoing.

Trojan Roofing is actively posting for Outside Sales Representatives, describing the company as rapidly growing.

Their pitch separates selling from project management — dedicated support staff handle logistics so reps stay in the field.

Indeed currently lists 31 open jobs for Trojan Roofing

— an unusually high number for a single-market contractor and a red flag for churn, not just growth. No marketing, operations leadership, or digital roles were identified. The hiring pipeline is almost exclusively commission-based field sales, which telegraphs their go-to-market: feet on the street after storms, not inbound digital.

4. Off-Google Presence

The off-Google picture is damaging and largely unmanaged.

Their BBB profile carries an F rating for failure to respond to 5 filed complaints, with advertising issues also flagged.

One BBB complaint details a customer who agreed to work with Trojan after they canvassed the neighborhood post-hail damage, only to report the company "made every attempt to avoid providing a written agreement."

Their Yelp listing exists but is functionally a ghost — no reviews visible and messaging disabled.

Their Facebook page has only 233 likes

with no evidence of paid Ad Library activity.

Indeed employee reviews from January 2026 describe management as directionless and cite an instance of an unpaid final paycheck.

The Google-vs.-BBB gap (4.9 stars on Google, F on BBB) is a textbook review-funneling pattern — satisfied customers routed to Google, complaints absorbed quietly.

7. Strategic Read vs. Raptor

Where they beat Raptor: Raw field sales throughput — 31 open roles and a canvassing-first model means they are likely knocking more doors post-storm than any other single competitor in Marion County. Their review velocity when active has been strong. Where Raptor beats them: Every dimension that matters to a discerning homeowner. Raptor holds 699 reviews at a perfect 5.0 vs. Trojan's 638 at 4.9 — but the more exploitable gap is operational credibility:

Trojan carries an F on BBB with 5 unanswered complaints

, a 0.0% owner response rate on Google, and a trail of negative employee reviews describing a chaotic, commission-only churn machine. Most exploitable weakness: Their BBB F rating and documented complaint pattern around written agreements is a direct contrast point Raptor can own in sales conversations and trust-building content without ever naming the competitor.

8. What Raptor Should Watch or Copy

  1. Ops/sales separation as a recruiting pitch.

Trojan's job ads explicitly promise reps they won't juggle project management — dedicated support staff handle logistics.

Whether they deliver on it is debatable (see employee reviews), but the messaging resonates with sales talent. Cameron should consider whether Raptor's recruiting materials make that same structural promise as clearly.

  1. Storm canvassing surge capacity. Trojan's model of posting 31 open sales roles simultaneously is designed for rapid post-storm deployment. Raptor should have a documented storm-response playbook — pre-approved ad creative, pre-written canvassing scripts, a bench of trained part-time inspectors — ready to activate within 48 hours of a hail event, before Trojan's reps flood the same neighborhoods.

  2. Monitor their review trajectory as a leading indicator. Trojan pulled only 1 review in the last 90 days after building 638 total. That is a company either contracting, distracted, or losing its review-collection infrastructure to turnover.

Customers have also noted inconsistent communication and difficulty reaching the company across multiple representatives.

If Raptor sees Trojan's review pace pick back up suddenly, that signals a management reset worth tracking.
